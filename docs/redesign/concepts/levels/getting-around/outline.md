# Getting around: outline of the level (second version)

This is an outline only. Nothing has been built, and every word that a child would read is still to be written by somebody else. This version replaces the first one, which was too close to the Power and School outlines.

## The place, and what makes it look unlike Homes

The level is set at the row of shops in the middle of the neighbourhood, on a bright spring day when every tree is in pink and white blossom. There is a baker, a greengrocer and a bicycle repair shop, with striped awnings, crates of fruit and a paved forecourt in front. The child's family waits on the forecourt for Gran, who lives in the furthest home and is coming to do the shopping with them. Homes is a long green street of houses in summer and a snowy night. This level is a busy shopfront in spring colours of pink, cream, fresh green and awning red, and the blossom makes it plain at once where trees were kept and where they were cut.

## The camera, the time of day and the weather

It is late morning, the sun is high, the shadows are short and petals drift down, with no wind. The camera sits just above the awning, at about the height of a first-floor window, with the family large in the foreground below and the streets or paths running away from the shops in front of them. This is low enough to see faces and high enough to see the shape of the streets. The three plans are shown one after another from this same spot, never side by side.

## What the child sees when "Open the shops" is pressed

1. The shutters roll up, the awnings come out, the baker carries out a tray of bread and the shop bell rings.
2. The view shows the grid plan. Straight streets cross each other and run away from the shops. Gran and the other neighbours walk in along them and fill the forecourt, but the streets are bare, with stumps where the trees were.
3. A camera shutter clicks and a postcard of that moment drops into Florence's band.
4. The view changes to the winding loop. A wide park in blossom lies in front of the shops, but no path crosses it, and the one street bends away out of sight. The near neighbours walk in. Gran and the others from the far homes arrive by car, and the cars park across the forecourt and hide the shops. A second postcard drops into the band.
5. The view changes to the paths through the park. Paths fan out from the shop doors straight under the blossom to the homes. Gran walks in with everyone else, the forecourt fills with people, and the cars pass on the outside road far behind. A third postcard drops into the band.

The answer shows at a glance in the three postcards side by side. The child looks for two things in each one, which are whether the space in front of the shops is full of people or full of parked cars, and whether there is blossom or stumps. The child can click a postcard to see that morning again. The result cards follow, each with its postcard above the sentences and marks for "Promote walking" and "Nature".

## The play break: fit the paths together

1. The camera moves to a close, slanted view of the ground between the homes and the shops, marked out in large squares like a board game. Trees, a pond and a stream stand on some squares, and nothing can be put on those.
2. Florence's band holds three kinds of path piece: a straight piece, a bend and a fork. The child clicks a piece, the squares where it can go turn yellow, and the child clicks a square to lay it. The piece turns itself to join the path, and where it could face two ways the child clicks it again to turn it.
3. As soon as a home is joined to the shops, the person from that home walks the new path to the baker's door, so the child sees at once that the path works.
4. There are fewer pieces than squares. The child therefore decides which way to go round the old tree, where to use the forks so that one path serves two homes, and which of two places gets the one bridge over the stream.
5. Any piece can be picked up and laid again, and nothing is ever marked as wrong. The child also chooses the colour of the paving.
6. At the end the camera drops to the path and the family walks the child's route once, under the blossom, to the shops.

If the child chose the grid, the board is bare streets, and the pieces are pavements and crossings with a few young trees to plant. If the child chose the loop, pieces may only be laid along the loop, so the far homes stay a long way round. The activity teaches that the shape of the paths decides how far people walk, that paths which join can serve many homes, and that a path can bend round a tree.

## The mark the choice leaves on the town

The town is afterwards drawn with the chosen plan and with the child's own pattern of paths, bridge and paving colour in it. People walk on those paths in later levels.

## How long it should take

The level should take about ten minutes. The job and the sentence take two minutes, the plans and the test take two and a half, choosing takes one, the play break takes three, and the town, "Try another plan" and the recap tap take the rest.

## The two biggest risks

The first risk is that three mornings shown in turn are harder to compare than three things shown together. The postcards must therefore be large and sharp, and the difference between a forecourt of people and a forecourt of cars must be readable in a small picture.

The second risk is that fitting pieces is fiddly for the youngest children, or has one obvious answer. There are only three kinds of piece, they turn themselves, and the board needs at least two good patterns that look different. The play break also needs a board for each of the three plans, which is the largest piece of building in the level.
