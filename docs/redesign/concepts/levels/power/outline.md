# Level 5, Power: outline for the owner

This is an outline only. Nothing has been built. Any words quoted for the screen are placeholders.

## The place, and what makes it look unlike Homes

The level takes place on the windy hilltop above Eco Town, at a small wooden lookout beside the foot of a wind turbine. The child's family stand at the rail in coats and scarves, and the younger child flies a kite, so the wind can be seen without a word. The town lies below in the valley, with the river, the fields and a far ridge behind it. Homes is a closed, leafy street seen from the pavement. This level is open sky and distance: more than half of the picture is sky, the grass is pale with frost, and the colours are orange, pink and deep blue instead of green and brick.

## The camera, the time of day and the weather

The camera is at shoulder height on the lookout, just behind the family, looking out and slightly down over the valley. The family are large in the foreground, so the view is among people and not above them. The level starts late on a clear winter afternoon with a low orange sun, long shadows and a strong wind. There are patches of snow on the ground, but no snow falls, because Homes already has falling snow. The level never goes down into a street at night. It ends at a pink dawn, not in the dark.

## What the child sees when "Keep the lights on" is pressed

Before the press, the valley holds three neighbourhoods side by side, divided by the river and a hedge, and each one is run by one of Florence's plans. The first has solar panels on every roof. The second has a long row of turbines on the ridge behind it. The third has a few turbines, some solar roofs and a battery barn with an open front that shows ten large glowing cells. Power is drawn as beads of light that travel along raised lines from each source to the homes. There are no wires or sockets within anyone's reach.

1. The child presses the button. The sun sinks behind the far ridge in about eight seconds and the sky turns from orange to deep blue.
2. Families walk home along the lanes, and the windows in all three neighbourhoods switch on.
3. In the solar neighbourhood the beads of light thin out and stop, and the windows go dark street by street.
4. In the wind neighbourhood the kite still pulls, the turbines still turn, the beads keep flowing and the lights stay on. A flock of geese crosses the last of the sunset and has to bend its path around the long row of turbines.
5. In the third neighbourhood the lights stay on, and the cells in the battery barn go out one at a time as beads leave them, with a large number counting down beside them. The geese fly straight past, because the few turbines stand to one side.
6. The moon crosses the sky quickly, dawn comes, and the picture holds. The whole test takes about twenty-five seconds and can be played again.

The answer shows at a glance, because one neighbourhood is dark and two are bright, the geese bend around only one of them, and the battery barn has visibly emptied. The result cards from the shared kit then give a sentence for each check with its tick, dash or cross.

## The play break: run one winter day by hand

The camera moves closer to the neighbourhood that the child chose, still from the hill. The low path of the winter sun is drawn across the sky as a dotted arc.

1. The child sets up the plan. For the plan with wind, sun and a battery, the child places three turbines on five yellow places on the ridge, all of them away from the geese, and picks the colour of the stripe on the battery barn. For the other plans the child places the panels on the roofs or the turbines on the ridge in the same way.
2. The child takes hold of the sun and drags it along its arc. Time moves only when the child moves it, so there is no timer, and the sun can be dragged back.
3. At midday the panels and the turbines make more beads than the homes need. The spare beads wait in a tray, and the child drags them into the empty cells of the battery barn. Each cell lights up and the number on the barn rises.
4. The child drags the sun below the ridge. The solar beads stop, some homes go dark, and the child drags beads out of the battery into those homes, which light up with a person waving at the window.
5. The child drags the moon across to dawn. The first new beads arrive from the sun, and the family on the lookout cheer.

The child's hands do three things: place, drag the sun, and move spare power into the battery and out again. This teaches that the sun and the wind do not make power at the same times as people need it, and that a battery carries spare power from the day into the night. With the solar plan there is no battery to fill, so the child finds that the night stays dark, and Florence offers "Try another plan". Nobody is told that they are wrong. After about three minutes Florence offers to finish the day for the child.

## The mark the choice leaves on the town

The turbines stand on the ridge in the places the child chose and can be seen on the skyline from the rest of the town. The solar roofs glint, and the battery barn carries the child's colour and shows its ten cells. The closing picture is the family on the hill at dawn with the town twinkling below and the geese overhead.

## How long it should take

The whole level should take about ten minutes: two minutes for the job and the sentence, two for the plans and the test, one and a half for the results and the choice, three for the play break, and one and a half for the town and the recap tap.

## The two biggest risks

The first risk is that a town with lit windows after dark still feels like the end of Homes. I answer this by staying on the hill, by keeping the view wide, and by running from sunset to dawn. The cost of the wide view is that the homes are small, so the windows and the people in them must be drawn much larger than life to be readable, and that must be checked in the first render.

The second risk is in the play break. Dragging the sun and moving beads may be fiddly with a mouse, and every number on the screen must add up. The break is also thinner for the solar plan and the wind plan, which have no battery to work. There is a related point in the approved results: the wind plan keeps the lights on all night, so the test does not show that a battery is needed, and only the play break teaches it. The owner may wish to decide whether that is enough.
