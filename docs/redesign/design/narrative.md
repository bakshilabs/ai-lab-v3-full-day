# The story of the hour in Eco Town

This document is a narrative design review of the six Eco Town levels in the redesign. It was written on 22 November 2026 from the wireframe words (`docs/redesign/wireframes-data.js`, the first town in the list), the film's copy of the town (`docs/redesign/animation/town.js`, `eco.js` and `film.js`), the curriculum, the feedback files and the owner's rules in `CLAUDE.md`.

This document does not contain any new sentence for children, and it does not contain sample dialogue. Where it quotes words, they are words that are already in the data file, quoted so that the lead can find them. Every recommendation describes what a screen or a film shot should do, and the words themselves are left to whoever writes them under the owner's rules. The owner's page for editing the wireframe words (the `words` collection) held no edits when this was written, so the data file is the current wording.

The owner has discussed Astra's suggestion that the six levels should feel like one town, and has not yet approved changes for it. Everything below is therefore a proposal. Section 10 lists the points that need the owner's decision before anyone acts on them.

## 1. A summary of what I found

The six levels already work well one at a time. Each one has a clear need, one test and a visible result. What is missing is the feeling that they are six chapters of one story. At the moment each level opens as if the town had no history, and several levels describe a town that the child did not build.

The largest problems are these.

1. The hour never tells the child who they are in this story, and the opening describes a town that already exists, when the film and the first level both show empty land.
2. The words of later levels assume the choices of the sample child. Three levels mention a hill, and the last level assumes farm families who live far out. A child who chose differently would read words that do not match their town.
3. Some things are spoken of before they exist. Level 2 sends people to the shops and names a school, but no level builds shops, and the school is not built until level 6.
4. Some marks that the child makes are later removed or contradicted. The first homes disappear in the film when the timber homes arrive, the school level describes the park at the edge of town after the child put it in the middle, and week three floods a street of a town that the child built to stay dry.
5. The end of the hour goes from the last level straight to a list of jobs. The child never looks back at the whole town, and the talking question asks what the child gave up, although the owner's rule is now that one plan in every level is good on every check.

The rest of this document explains these points and proposes a story structure that fixes them without changing the flow of a level.

## 2. The through-line of the hour

### Who the child is

The child should be the planner of a new town. People are coming to live on this land, and they are relying on the child to decide where and how the town is built. Florence is the child's helper, who suggests plans. The child tests the plans and makes every decision. This matches the owner's rule that Florence suggests and the child decides, and it matches the jobs on the last screen, where the first job is a town planner. The hour should begin by giving the child this role, and it should end by showing that real people hold the same role.

At the moment the role is never stated. The opening says "We are going to plan it together so that it is a good place to live", which is a good statement of the goal, but it does not say for whom the child is planning or why the child is the one who decides.

### What is at stake

The stake should be the people. A small number of families arrive on empty land, more follow, and by the end of the hour a whole town lives there. Each level is one thing that those people need next: a safe place, a way to get about, homes, food, power in winter, and a school for their children. The question in the child's mind in every level should be whether the people will be all right, and the test in every level already answers that question in a way a child can see: the homes stay dry, everyone walks to the shops, the lights stay on, every child walks to school.

Nature is the second stake, and it should be present from the first screen, not introduced in level 4. The bees and flowers are already in the film's town from the start. The child should feel that the land had a life before the town arrived, and that a good planner keeps it.

### What changes from the first screen to the last

The first screen should show empty land with a river, a meadow and a hill, and no buildings. The last screen should show the same land, from the same direction, with a living town on it: homes with lit windows, people on the paths, a cart on the road, turbines turning, children outside the school, and the meadow still full of bees. The child should be able to point to six things in that picture and say that each one is there because of a decision they made. That before-and-after picture is the story of the hour, and the film should be built around it.

### A light shape in time

The levels already suggest the passing of a year. Level 1 has heavy rain. Level 3 says that the town has cold winters, which looks ahead. Level 5 takes place on a winter evening. Level 6 opens with a school bell, which feels like a morning. I recommend treating the hour as the first year of the town, shown only through light and weather and never explained in words. This gives the film a reason to change the sky between levels, and it makes the winter night of level 5 feel like something the town has been heading towards since level 3. This is an addition to the look of the game, so it is listed in section 10 for the owner.

## 3. How each level should follow from the one before

The principle is simple. The need of each level should be caused by the result of the level before it. The child should feel that the next job has arrived because the town has grown, and not because the game has moved to the next item on a list.

The first screen of every level must still ask the child to ask Florence to suggest plans and then to test them, as the owner has ruled. The link to the earlier level belongs in the situation at the start of that first screen (the `job` text), in the film shot that comes before it, or in the town itself. The invitation above the word buttons (`scene`) should stay short, as the owner has asked.

I recommend at most one spoken link to the past in each level. Everything else should be carried by what the town shows. Children of this age notice what is on the screen far more reliably than what is said, and the feedback from the eight-year-old player shows this: the moment she responded to was seeing that the bees had returned.

### Level 1, Land

This level has no earlier level to follow. Its situation should follow from the opening: people are coming, the land is empty, and rain is on the way. The existing invitation, "Heavy rain is coming. Where could we put the homes?", does this well.

The level should leave behind the first few homes on the chosen land and the first family in them. It should also leave the two places that were not chosen looking as they did, because both come back later. The meadow becomes the wild place of level 4, and the river remains the edge of the map.

### Level 2, Getting around

The opening should rest on the fact that people now live in the place the child chose. The need arises because the first families have arrived and have nowhere to walk yet. The level should be set on the child's own site, with the first homes in view.

The level should leave behind the paths, the park and its trees, the road, and a town centre. The town centre matters for the story, and section 6 explains why.

### Level 3, Homes

The opening should rest on the town having become a place that people want to move to, now that it is dry and easy to walk around. The existing situation, "Forty families are moving to Eco Town", is the right idea. The new homes should visibly stand along the streets that the child laid out in level 2, so that the streets are seen to have been worth planning.

This level also plants the winter. The line "Eco Town has cold winters" is the only place where an earlier level sets up a later one, and it should be kept for that reason.

### Level 4, Food

The opening should rest on the fact that the town is now much bigger. Forty more families need to eat. The film should make this visible: many more people in the streets, and a centre with nothing to sell yet.

This level should recall level 1 through the land. The wild meadow that the farms could take over should be recognisably the meadow that the child looked at in level 1. In the film's town it already is the same piece of low land, with flowers and bees on it from the start, but the words never connect the two. The cart that carries the food should travel on the road that the child built in level 2 and arrive at the centre from level 2.

### Level 5, Power

The opening should rest on the homes from level 3 and the winter that level 3 promised. The season has turned, the evening is dark, and the homes that the child chose are the ones whose windows are in question. Solar panels should be seen going on to the child's own roofs, which the film already does. The birds in the Nature check of this level should be recognisably the birds that the child protected in level 4, in the wild places that the child kept.

### Level 6, A school for everyone

This is the level where every earlier choice should be on screen at once, because the test sends children out of every home and along every path. The children leave the homes of level 3 and the farm houses of level 4, they walk on the paths of level 2 and the cart road of level 4, and the bell rings in a town whose lights the child kept on in level 5. The existing words already point at this with "Where the school is close and the paths join up, the children walk." That sentence is the strongest callback in the current data, and the level should lean on it.

The situation at the start already recalls level 4 ("some live far out by the farms"), which is the right kind of link, although its wording has a consistency problem that section 6 describes.

## 5. The townspeople

### Their role

The townspeople are the reason for every job and the measure of every test. They should never speak, because Florence is the only voice in the game and the game uses no names. They should act. They arrive, they walk, they carry things, they switch on lights, and they show plainly how they feel about what has happened to them. A family that stays dry in the rain, or a child who walks to school with friends, tells the child that the plan worked more warmly than a tick does. The ticks should stay, as the owner has decided, underneath what the child sees.

### A few recurring figures

I recommend a small cast of figures that the child can recognise by colour and by one object each, with no names and no words. Four is enough, and more would be lost on the screen at this scale.

1. The first family. They arrive on the empty land in the opening and move into one of the first homes in level 1. One of them is a child in a coat of a colour that no other figure wears. This child is the thread of the hour, because the last level is about children.
2. The child in the bright coat. This figure walks to the shops beside a parent in level 2, watches the new families arrive in level 3, is at the market when the cart comes in level 4, is seen at a lit window in level 5, and is the first child out of the door when the bell rings in level 6.
3. The farmer with the cart. The film's town already has a figure in blue who walks with the food cart. This figure should arrive in level 4 and keep making the same journey for the rest of the hour, and the farmer's own children should be among those who walk to school in level 6.
4. The wildlife of the meadow. The bees are already in the film from the start. Together with the birds they act as a fifth townsperson. They should be seen before any building begins, they should be in danger in level 4, and they should still be there in the last picture.

These figures should live in the town views and in the film's shots between levels. They may also appear in the three small test scenes of each level, so that it is the child's own townspeople who walk or drive in each plan, but that is a larger piece of drawing and is not necessary for the story to work.

One caution is needed. The figures must not become a reason to add reading. They should carry no speech bubbles and no labels.

## 6. Consistency problems in the current data

Each item below names the words that cause the problem and says why they work against the single-town story. The items are in order of how much they would confuse a child.

1. **The opening describes a town that is already there.** The opening says "This is a small town with homes, farms and a river." The film opens on empty land, level 1 asks the child to choose where the town will be built, the homes arrive in levels 1 and 3, and the farms arrive in level 4. The opening should describe the land and the people who are coming, and not a finished town. This sentence came from Oliver's feedback, where it replaced "a low town", so the change needs the owner's agreement.

2. **Three levels assume that the child chose the hill.** Level 3 offers the word buttons "on the shady side of the hill", "on the sunny side of the hill" and "on top of the windy hill". Level 5 says "the wind still blows on the hill" and offers the plan "Wind farm on the hill", described as "on the hill above the town". None of these makes sense for a child who built the town on the meadow or on the river bank. The phrase "above the town" is also odd for the sample child, whose town is itself on the hill. Section 10 asks the owner how the game should deal with a child who makes a different choice in level 1.

3. **Level 6 assumes farm families who live far out.** The situation says "Some families live in the centre, and some live far out by the farms." The sample child chose farms that are "a short walk from the town", so "far out" contradicts the choice that was just made. The words of level 4 also never say that anyone lives at the farms. The film adds two farm houses in level 4, which is the right answer, and the words of level 4 should support it. A child who chose the far farms in level 4 would face a different school problem again.

4. **Level 2 names places that do not exist yet.** The situation says "People need to get to the shops, the school and the park." The school is the subject of level 6, so it should not be a destination in level 2. No level builds shops, and the film's town has no shops in it. The test is called "Open the shops", so the shops matter. I recommend that the town has one centre from level 2 onwards, shown in the town when the paths are built, and that the same place is the market of level 4 and the centre of level 6. At present the data calls it "the shops" in level 2, "the market" in level 4, and "the middle of town, next to the shops" in level 6, and a child cannot tell that these are one place. In the film's town the market stall first appears in level 4 at the edge of the hill, and nothing stands where the paths cross.

5. **Level 6 moves the park.** The second plan of level 6 is "Beside the park", described as "at the edge of town, with the park next door." In level 2 the sample child put the park "in the middle of the town", and the plan that was built is "Paths through a park" in the middle of town. The school plan should place the park where the child put it.

6. **The first homes vanish.** In the film's town the four homes built in level 1 shrink away when the timber homes of level 3 arrive. The child's very first mark on the town is erased without a word. The first homes should stay as the oldest homes in the town, and the forty families should be seen to join them. If the look of the town requires all homes to match, then the first homes should visibly be rebuilt in the chosen material, so that the change is seen as part of the child's choice.

7. **Week three floods the town that was built to stay dry.** The last level of Robot Farm begins "The river has flooded a street in Eco Town, and people need supplies." The whole achievement of level 1 is that the town "stays dry when the river rises." A child who remembers their first decision will feel that the game has undone it. The flood rescue should happen somewhere that the child did not protect, for example another town or the low land by the river, and this needs the owner's decision because it changes the words of another week.

8. **Level 3 promises warmth and tests something else.** The invitation says "Eco Town has cold winters. Let's help the homes stay warm." The word buttons are about which side of the hill and which way the windows face. Florence's three plans then differ only in material, the test is "Make the materials", which shows pollution, and all three plans are good at staying warm. The child's sentence therefore has no visible effect on the plans, and the question that the invitation raised is never tested. As a story, the level sets up one thing and pays off another. The warmth is paid off in level 5, which is good, but level 3 itself should make clear at the start that the question is how the homes are made. This touches the owner's rule that word buttons must be a real decision, so it is listed in section 10.

9. **Two closing lines describe a loss that the story never returns to.** After level 1 Florence says that "people have a longer walk down to the water", and after level 2 that "cars have to drive the long way round." These lines come from the earlier design in which every plan gave something up. Under the current rule the sample child's plan is good on every check, and nothing later in the hour is about the walk to the water. Each line leaves a small loose end at the moment when the child should feel that the job is done.

10. **The talking question asks about something that no longer happens.** The question for the teacher's discussion is "Which choice was the hardest to make in Eco Town, and what did you give up when you made it?" A child who chose the plan that was good on every check gave nothing up. The second half of the question belongs to the earlier design.

11. **The line before a second try contradicts a good choice.** The line that offers another try says "Your plan works in some ways." It is shown after the sample child has chosen the plan that is good on every check, so it tells the child that a fully good plan is only partly good.

12. **The low land that flooded becomes the farmland.** In level 1 the test shows that "Water spreads across the low land." In the film's town the farms of level 4 are then built on low land. Real farms are often on land that floods, so this is not wrong, but a child who remembers level 1 may ask about it, and the teacher should be ready for the question. It needs no change unless the owner wants one.

13. **The opening line about Florence leans towards the lesson that the owner removed.** The line that introduces Florence ends "you must check whether they work." The owner has ruled that the redesign should not carry over the idea that Florence could be wrong. The line can be read either way. The two recap questions that ask whether Florence included everything the child asked for (levels 2 and 4) have the same slight lean. For the story it is cleaner if Florence is plainly a capable helper and the tests are plainly about the plans and the town, and not about Florence. This is the owner's call.

14. **The last line of the last level looks away from the town.** After level 6 the closing words end "Car trips create pollution and traffic delays." The final thing that the child hears inside the story is a general fact about cars. The final beat should be about the child's own town and its children.

## 7. The opening and the end of the hour

### The opening

The opening should do four things, in this order, and should do them mostly in pictures.

1. It should show the whole world and then come down to one piece of empty land. The film's opening already does this, starting from the six-town map. This is valuable because it sets up next week before the hour has begun.
2. It should show the land alive before anyone builds on it: the river, the meadow with its bees, the hill.
3. It should show the first people arriving, so that the child sees for whom they are planning.
4. It should give the child their role as the planner and introduce Florence as the helper who suggests plans.

The opening currently also shows a list of all five checks before the child has done anything. As a matter of story, this is a list of rules read out before the tale has begun, and four of the five checks are not used in level 1. Each check would be better met in the level where it first matters. This would change a screen that the owner has reviewed, so it is in section 10.

### The end of the hour

At present the hour ends with the level 6 recap, then the jobs, then the talking question, then the line about the break. The story needs one more beat before the jobs: a look back at the town. I recommend this order.

1. The look back. The view returns to the direction of the very first shot and shows the finished town, so that the child sees the before and the after. The six things that the child decided should be shown one after another, in the order that they were decided. The shot should end at dusk with every window lit, which is the town's most complete and most rewarding state. The townspeople should be out, and the child in the bright coat should be among them.
2. The jobs. The jobs should arrive as the answer to the question of who does this work in the real world, directly after the child has seen their own work whole. The existing line "People do this kind of work every day when they plan real towns" has exactly the right intent.
3. The talk with the teacher. The question should invite the child to tell the story of their own town. It should not ask what was given up.
4. The bridge to next week, described below.

## 8. The bridge to the next town

The five later towns already refer to Eco Town. Drone City plans "flights that help all six towns", and its third level delivers medicine to "the landing pad in Eco Town". Robot Farm's last level takes place in an Eco Town street. Power Ridge sends power "to all six towns". The world is clearly meant to be one world, and the bridge should make the child feel that before they leave.

I recommend three things.

1. The last shot of the hour should pull back from the finished town to the world map, the same map that opened the hour. The child's town should be visible on it as they built it, and the other five towns should be seen waiting. The camera should end facing the next town.
2. Something from the next town should be seen crossing towards Eco Town in that last shot. For Drone City the natural choice is a drone in the sky, since the map already has a drone flying a delivery to Eco Town. Nothing needs to be said. The existing line "The next town opens next week" can stay as it is.
3. Next week should open by leaving from the child's Eco Town, so that the same shot is seen in reverse. When a later level takes place in Eco Town, it should take place in the town as the child built it.

The third point depends on the game remembering each child's town between weeks, which is a decision for the owner. Two small matters follow from it. The delivery level needs a landing pad in Eco Town, and no Eco Town level builds one, so the pad should either appear at the end of the hour as the town's first link to the wider world, or the delivery level should say that it has been added. Power Ridge's claim to power all six towns also sits beside Eco Town making its own power in level 5, and the story of week six should allow for that.

## 9. What the film should show between levels

At present the film cuts between levels with a wipe, a title card and a camera move from the last level's view to the next. The camera move is good, because it keeps the child in one place. The wipe works against the single-town feeling, because a wipe tells the viewer that one thing has ended and a different thing has begun.

I recommend that between levels the film stays in the town with no wipe, and uses four to six seconds for a wordless shot in two halves. The first half shows the town enjoying the last choice. The second half shows the next need arriving. The wipe should be kept only for moving to and from the table with the three test scenes, where the child really does leave the town for a moment.

| Between | The first half: the last choice is enjoyed | The second half: the next need arrives |
|---|---|---|
| The opening and level 1 | The empty land is alive with bees and birds. | The first family arrives, and rain clouds gather. |
| Levels 1 and 2 | The rain has passed and the first homes are dry. The family comes out of the door. | They look across the hill and have no path to follow. |
| Levels 2 and 3 | People stroll on the new paths and the park is green. | Many new families arrive at the edge of the town with their belongings, and there are too few homes. |
| Levels 3 and 4 | The new families move in along the streets. | The town centre is busy with people and has no food to offer them. |
| Levels 4 and 5 | The cart comes up the road to the centre, and the bees are busy in the meadow. | The days grow short, the sun sinks early, and the windows of the homes are dark. |
| Levels 5 and 6 | The town glows through the winter night. | Morning comes, and children step out of their doors, in the town and at the farms, with nowhere to go. |
| Level 6 and the end | The children walk to school together along the paths. | The look back described in section 7, ending at dusk, and then the pull back to the world map. |

Three further points apply to the film.

1. The three test scenes of each level are drawn on separate plates of land. They should look like three copies of the child's town as it stands, with the earlier choices on each plate, so that the child understands that the test is being run on their own town.
2. The film should show the sample child's town changing its light across the hour if the owner accepts the idea of the first year.
3. The first homes should stay in the film from level 3 onwards, for the reason given in section 6.

## 10. Points that need the owner's decision

These are listed separately because each one either changes something that the owner has already ruled on, or adds work that the owner has not asked for.

1. **Should the six levels be told as one town's story at all?** This is Astra's suggestion, which the owner has discussed and not yet approved. Everything else in this document depends on the answer.
2. **What happens to a child who does not choose the sample child's plan?** The later levels are written for the hill, the park in the middle, timber homes, farms close by, and wind with sun and a battery. The choices are these. The first is that the town keeps whatever the child chose, and the words of later levels are written so that they are true for any choice. The second is that the words of later levels change with the earlier choice. The third is that the game leads every child to the plan that is good on every check before moving on. I recommend the first, because it keeps the child's choice real and needs no extra writing, but it means removing the hill from the words of levels 3 and 5 and the phrase "far out" from level 6.
3. **Should a poor choice stay visible?** If a child keeps the town beside the river, the question is whether later levels show a town on a worn bank. Keeping it honours the rule that every choice leaves a mark. It also means that some children carry a spoiled town for the hour. This is a question about how kind the game should be, and only the owner can answer it.
4. **How many versions of the town should be drawn?** Showing each child's own choices in later levels multiplies the drawings: three sites, three street plans, three kinds of home, three farm plans, three power plans and three schools. The owner should decide whether all are drawn, or only the plans that are good on every check together with a simpler picture for the others.
5. **May the opening sentence change?** The sentence "This is a small town with homes, farms and a river" came from Oliver's feedback and was agreed. It describes a town that does not yet exist.
6. **Should the five checks be introduced one at a time?** This would replace the opening screen that lists all five.
7. **Should the talking question change?** The words "what did you give up" belong to the earlier design.
8. **Should the hour be shown as the first year of the town?** This affects only light and weather.
9. **Should the townspeople include four recurring figures?** They would have no names and no words. The question is whether the drawing work is wanted now.
10. **Should the flood rescue in week three move out of the child's town?** This changes the words of Robot Farm.
11. **Should the game remember each child's town for later weeks?** The bridge in section 8 is strongest if it does.
12. **Should level 3 be reshaped so that its opening matches its test?** This touches the owner's rule about word buttons.
13. **Should the line that introduces Florence, and the two recap questions about her, be reviewed against the rule that Florence is not to be caught out?**
14. **Who writes any new or changed words?** The owner's permission for Claude to write words covers the wireframes only. The film and the game may be covered by the rule that the words come from OpenAI.

## 11. Recommendations in order of importance

The order is by how much each one adds to the feeling of one story for the work that it needs.

1. Give the child a role and give the hour a before and an after. The opening shows empty land and arriving people and names the child as the planner. The end returns to the same view of the finished town.
2. Make the need of each level follow from the result of the level before, as set out in section 3, with at most one spoken link in each level and the rest shown.
3. Remove the words that assume the sample child's choices, or decide how the game handles other choices (section 6, items 2 and 3, and section 10, item 2).
4. Give the town one centre from level 2 onwards, and use the same place as the shops, the market and the middle of town. Take the school out of the list of places in level 2.
5. Keep every mark that the child makes. The first homes stay, the park stays where the child put it, and the town that was built to stay dry is not flooded in week three.
6. Add the look back before the jobs at the end of the hour, and change the talking question so that it invites the child to tell their town's story.
7. Replace the wipes between levels in the film with the two-part wordless shots in section 9.
8. Use the land of level 1 again in level 4, so that the meadow that was not built on is the wild place that the child protects.
9. Add the four recurring figures, starting with the child in the bright coat, in the town views and the shots between levels.
10. End the hour by pulling back to the world map with something from the next town on its way, and open next week from the child's own Eco Town.
