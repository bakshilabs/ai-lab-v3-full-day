# Learning design review of the six Eco Town levels

This review was written on 22 November 2026 by a learning designer for children aged 8 to 12. It looks at the six Eco Town levels as they stand in `docs/redesign/wireframes-data.js`, so that the lead can build the animated film and, later, the game.

I read the owner's rules in `CLAUDE.md`, the curriculum, the full-day plan, the wireframe words and page, the feedback from Oliver, Alan Harrison and the parent of the eight-year-old, and the three rounds of proposals. I also read the `words` collection in the artifact database, and it was empty on 22 November, so the file is the current wording. I have not changed any file other than this one, and I have not written or reworded any sentence that a child reads. Where a sentence has a problem, I point to it and say what it needs to achieve.

I have treated the owner's rules as settled. Where a rule has a consequence for learning, I describe how to work within the rule, and I do not ask for the rule to be changed.

## The short answer

The flow of every level is sound for this age group. A child asks, sees three plans, starts one test, reads what happened, and chooses. The tests are concrete, the results are sentences about what happened, and the child never has to read a rating without having seen something first. The Land level and the Food level are the strongest of the six.

There are four problems that matter more than the rest. The sentence that the child builds does not visibly change anything that Florence does. In five levels the best word buttons already name the winning plan, so the test confirms a decision and does not inform it. Two levels show science that a careful teacher would have to correct, which are Homes and Power. The recap taps mostly check that the child was looking, and they do not check that the child understood the cause.

## What a child learns in each level

### Level 1, Land

A child learns that land which is far from a river can still flood if it is low, and that height is what keeps a town dry. This is a true and useful idea, and it is the best piece of subject learning in the hour, because most children expect "far away" to be enough. The raised bank that is worn away by the river is also honest.

About working with Florence, the child learns the routine. That is the right aim for a first level.

The weakness is that the word buttons and the plans are the same three things. The child taps the words for high and far away, and Florence then offers a place beside the river, a place on the far meadow and a place on the far hill. A child who has just asked for high land will reasonably wonder why Florence offered the river bank. The data does not say what Florence offers if the child taps one of the other two buttons.

### Level 2, Getting around

A child learns that people walk when the walk is short, and that the layout of the streets decides how long the walk is. The child also meets two checks at once for the first time. Both are sound aims.

Two points need care. The grid of streets is given a cross for nature because most of the trees were cut down, but nothing in the description of the grid explains why a grid needs more trees to be cut than the other two plans. A child may conclude that straight streets are bad for nature, which is not a real rule. The film should show the cause, for example that the grid covers the place where the park would have been.

The recap asks the child to point to a plan where everyone walked. Two plans meet that description, which are the grid and the paths through the park. The game and the film must accept either answer, or the question must ask for something that only one plan did.

### Level 3, Homes

This level tries to teach two different things, and they do not meet. The word buttons are about where a home stands and which way its windows face. That is real knowledge about using the sun for warmth, and the choices are a real decision. The plans and the test are about something else, which is the material of the walls and the pollution from making it. Nothing on the screen ever shows what the sunny side or the windows did. A child who taps a poor button and a child who taps the best button see exactly the same level.

The "Staying warm" check gives a tick to all three plans. A check on which every plan scores the same teaches nothing about the choice, and here it is also not something the child sees happen, because the test button starts the sawmill, the oven and the cement works and does not test warmth at all. The sentence also promises homes that use little energy and last a long time, and neither is tested.

The science has four problems, which I list in the section on honesty below. The most important one is that the level says cutting down trees was bad in level 2 and that building with timber is the best choice in level 3, and it never explains the difference.

### Level 4, Food

This is the best-constructed level. One plan fails on wildlife, one plan fails on fresh food, and one plan passes both. A child has to read both checks to find the good plan, so the level really does teach that one check is not enough. The test shows both things happening, which are the cart or the lorry, and the bees staying or going.

The label of the second check is "Fresh food", and the results are about freshness and distance, not about pollution. That is the honest way to do it, because the distance food travels is only a small part of the pollution that food causes. The film should keep this level about freshness.

The recap in this level is only a question about Florence, and the question is hard to answer truthfully. It asks whether Florence left space for the bees and flowers, and the sample answer is yes. One of Florence's three plans put the farms on the wild meadow, so a thoughtful child could fairly answer no. The question also has two parts joined together with a negative in the second part, which is difficult for an eight-year-old.

### Level 5, Power

The aim is that a child learns why a town needs more than one source of clean power and somewhere to store the spare. That is a good aim, and it matches level 3 of the Power Ridge curriculum later on.

The test as written does not show this. The scene says that the wind still blows on the hill, and the wind farm keeps the lights on and receives a tick. The wind farm loses to the mixed plan only because birds had to fly around the turbines. So the evidence on the screen says that the mixed plan is better for birds, and it does not say that the mixed plan is more reliable. The lesson that the level is named for is never tested.

The recap then asks what kept the lights on after sunset, and the sample answer is the battery. In the scene the wind was blowing, so the turbines also kept the lights on. The answer also invites the idea that a battery makes power. Oliver warned about this on 21 November.

This level is also the heaviest to read. The results screen has nine sentences and 58 words.

### Level 6, A school for everyone

A child learns that where a shared building stands decides who can walk to it, and that a place in between serves children from both sides. The child also gives a reason for the choice for the first time, and the teacher can use that reason in the discussion.

This level is easier than levels 2 to 5. It has one gap, one check and three result sentences, and the best word button names the winning plan exactly. As the last level of the hour it does not ask more of the child than the first level did, apart from the reason. The two reasons that are not chosen describe the other two plans, so the reason is matched and not really reasoned.

## What a child learns about working with Florence

The four ideas are asking clearly, comparing suggestions, testing before deciding, and the person decides.

Comparing suggestions and testing before deciding are taught well, because they happen in every level and the child does them by hand. The words on the offers screen, which invite the child to test what happens with each plan, set the right tone. Florence does not know the answer yet either, and that keeps her a partner and avoids the idea that she is wrong.

Asking clearly is not taught yet. The child builds a sentence, but the game never shows that a different sentence would have produced different plans. This was the purpose of the step that the owner removed from level 4, and I agree that the step was too advanced. The purpose still needs a home inside "ask once". The simplest way is for Florence's plans to show that she heard the words. Each plan should visibly contain what the child asked for, and the level should define what Florence offers when a child taps a weaker button. The question in level 2 that asks whether Florence included everything the child asked for is the right kind of question, but it only means something if the answer can be seen in the town.

The person decides is present, but it is thin. Because every level has one plan that is good on every check, the decision is to find the column of ticks. I am not asking for that rule to change. It does mean that the learning has to come from explaining why the other plans failed, and not from the choice itself. The film, the recap and the discussion should all spend their time on the cause.

There is no growth in the AI learning from level 1 to level 6. The notes in the wireframes describe growth in the number of gaps and checks only. I recommend that each level is given one small aim about Florence that the film can show, in this order: the routine, then Florence used my words, then Florence gave me different kinds of plan, then two checks can disagree, then a test must be hard enough to be fair, and finally I can say why I chose.

## Does it match the curriculum?

The curriculum in `docs/curriculum.md` still says that Eco Town is about an AI that can be confidently wrong. The owner has since said that this idea must not be carried over. The curriculum document needs a new paragraph for Eco Town that states the new aim, and it should name the skill of each level as it does for the other five hours. I suggest these skills as a starting point for the owner: choosing a safe site, laying out streets for walking, choosing building materials, deciding how land is used, making clean power reliable, and placing a building that everyone shares.

The curriculum says that each mission opens with the real job in one line and ends with what the child can now do. The Eco Town levels do neither. The jobs appear only on the last screen.

The three jobs are right for the town, and they follow the owner's rule. The Food level has no matching job, and a farmer would be the natural one.

The full-day plan asks for three or four discussion questions about what the child did, what the machine needed from the child, and what the child would change. The wireframes have one question.

The curriculum expects about seven minutes for each level and about 45 minutes of play. With one ask, one test and one choice, I estimate that a quick twelve-year-old will finish a level in two to three minutes, and the six levels in under twenty. The length of the test animations will decide whether the hour is filled.

## Reading level and sentence length

I counted the words that a child reads in Eco Town. The typical sentence has nine or ten words, which is right for an eight-year-old. Six sentences have more than twenty words. Four of them are the sentences that the child builds, which run from 25 to 32 words in levels 2 to 5. A built sentence is read in pieces as the child taps, so it is less of a burden than it looks, but it should be spoken aloud once it is complete.

The opening screen has about 109 words, including five checks and their descriptions. That is too much to read before anything happens. The film should introduce each check when it is first used and not all five at the start.

The ask screens in levels 2 to 5 show between 59 and 70 words, because every button has several words on it. An eight-year-old reads these slowly. The job text of level 6 is 45 words in four sentences, which is the longest job in the town.

Some words will be new to many eight-year-olds: atmosphere, timber, concrete, turbine, solar panel, wildlife, materials, energy, direct and winding. Each of these is fine if the picture shows the thing at the moment the word is said. "Atmosphere" appears only in the description of the "Clean air" check and has no picture.

The name "Promote walking" is the owner's choice and I am not reopening it. I note only that an eight-year-old will rely on the sentence underneath it to understand it, so the film should always say the two together the first time.

The parent's report of 16 November showed an eight-year-old who could not follow written instructions without help. Every line from Florence should be spoken as well as shown, in both the film and the game.

For a twelve-year-old, the reading is easy, and the risk is the opposite one. The last two sentences of every job are the same in all six levels, and some weaker buttons are plainly silly, such as wasting the spare power. An older child will stop reading and tap. The Land, Homes and Food buttons need real thought, and the others should aim for that standard.

## How much a child must hold in mind

The levels with one check ask very little, and that is good. The levels with two checks and three plans show six result sentences, which is near the limit for an eight-year-old to compare. Level 5 shows nine.

Two things will decide whether this works. The first is how the three tests run. If all three plans are tested at the same moment in three parts of the town, an eight-year-old cannot watch them all. I recommend that the tests run one plan after another, always in the order A, B, C, and that each result sentence appears as its test ends and stays on the screen. The second is that each test button really shows only one check. Pressing "Make the materials" shows pollution and shows nothing about warmth. Pressing "Keep the lights on" shows the lights and shows nothing about pollution. The ticks for the other checks then appear without the child having seen anything, which is the very thing that Oliver's first point asked the design to avoid.

A check on which all three plans get the same result adds reading and adds nothing to the decision. "Staying warm" in Homes and "Clean air" in Power are both like this. Removing them from those two levels would cut Homes from six result sentences to three, and Power from nine to six.

The names of the checks change from level to level. The opening promises five, and the levels then show seven different names, because two are renamed. This is a small load, but it should be consistent in the film.

## Honesty of the science and the misconceptions that could form

In Homes, the watch text says that the sawmill runs on electricity and causes no pollution, and the result says that timber causes the least pollution. These two statements disagree. Electricity is clean only if it is made cleanly, and the town does not choose its power until level 5. The level needs to say "least" and never "none".

In Homes, timber means that trees are cut down. Level 2 gave a cross to the plan that cut down trees. A child will notice. The level needs to show honestly why timber can still be the better choice, which is that the trees are grown for the purpose and planted again.

In Homes, all three walls are said to keep homes warm for different reasons. Wool in a wall slows heat from leaving. A heavy concrete wall stores heat, but without a layer to slow the heat it lets warmth out quickly. Saying that all three stay warm equally is not accurate, and it is the claim a science teacher is most likely to challenge.

In Homes, the cloud from the cement works will look like dirty air to breathe. The owner has defined "Clean air" as pollution added to the atmosphere, so the film must not show people coughing or covering their faces. The teacher's discussion is the place to say what this pollution does.

In Power, the results say that the roofs, the turbines and the battery caused no pollution at all. One level earlier, the game counted the pollution from making things. Making panels, turbines and large batteries also causes pollution. The claim needs to be limited to the time when they are running.

In Power, the battery is at risk of being understood as a source of power. The level and its recap need to show the battery being filled before it is used.

In Power, the check is called "Staying warm" and the results are about lights. Oliver asked for this check to stay about warmth. The film should show the heating as well as the lights, or the level should use a name that fits what is seen.

In Land, the lesson is honest. In Food, the lesson is honest as long as it stays about freshness. In School and Getting around, the idea that everyone walks when the walk is short is a fair simplification, and the discussion can ask who might still find a short walk hard.

There are also three places where the story contradicts itself, and the film will make them visible. The town is built on the far hill a long way from the river, and level 2 then offers a park on the other side of the river. Level 3 offers the top of the windy hill and the sides of the hill as places for homes that already have a site. Level 4 mentions the far side of the island, which is the first time the child hears that the town is on an island.

## Do the recap taps check understanding?

They check attention more than understanding. In Land, Homes and School, the child points to the thing whose result sentence they have just read. Level 2 has two right answers. Level 4 has no tap at all. Level 5 asks about a cause, which is the right kind of question, but its answer is doubtful.

Because the answer is a tap and the recap never blocks progress, it is free to ask something harder. The aim of each recap should be that the child points to the cause and not to the winner. In Land the aim is to show what made the dry place dry. In Getting around the aim is to show what made the walk short. In Homes the aim is to show where the pollution came from. In Food the aim is to show what the bees needed. In Power the aim is to show where the stored power came from. In School the aim is to show whose walk changed. The words for these questions are for the owner's writing process, not for me.

Both questions about Florence have the answer yes. A question whose answer is always yes teaches children to tap yes.

## Sentences that need attention

The line after every result says that the plan works in some ways and offers another try. It comes from before the rule that the sample child chooses a plan that is good on every check. For that plan the line is untrue. It needs to invite curiosity about the other plans without saying that the chosen plan is lacking.

The discussion question asks what the child gave up. Under the rule that one plan is good on every check, the child gave up nothing. The Land and Getting around lines after the choice still mention a cost, which are a longer walk to the water and a longer drive for cars, and no check showed either cost. These lines and the discussion question need to agree with the rule.

The helper line says that the child must check whether Florence's ideas work. This fits the routine and does not say that Florence is wrong, so it is within the owner's rule. It should stay close to that meaning.

## What the teacher should aim for in the discussion

The discussion happens with screens down. I describe aims and not scripts.

The first aim is that children retell one test in their own words, saying what they saw happen and not which plan had ticks.

The second aim is that children explain a cause. The best two for this are why the far meadow flooded and why the solar roofs went dark.

The third aim is that children can say who did what. Florence suggested, the town showed what happened, and the child decided. The reasons saved from level 6 are the way into this.

The fourth aim is that children see that the game town is simpler than a real town. The teacher asks what the tests left out. This is where the simplifications are put right, such as a night with no wind, the pollution from making panels, and people who cannot walk far.

The fifth aim is to carry the ideas home. Children think about their own journey to school, or about where the water goes on their own street when it rains.

The sixth aim is the jobs. Children match each level to the person who does that work.

The teacher should also listen for three misunderstandings and correct them: that being far from a river is enough, that a battery makes power, and that anything causes no pollution at all.

## How the film could be used in a classroom

The film has three good uses, and one use to avoid.

It can be shown before play as a worked example. Only the Land level should be shown this way. It teaches the routine once, so that children do not need instructions in every level. Showing all six levels before play would give away every answer.

It can be shown after play as a shared recap, stopping at each level for one of the discussion aims.

It can replace the game in a classroom without devices. For this the film needs clear places to pause. The first pause is before the test button is pressed, when the class predicts what will happen to each plan. The second pause is before the choice, when the class votes. The third pause is at the recap. Predicting, watching and then explaining is the strongest way to use a film like this, and it suits both ages.

For any of these uses, the film should be divided into one chapter for each level, of about two minutes. Every line should be spoken and captioned. A sentence should stay on the screen for at least as long as it takes to read it aloud twice. The tests should run one plan at a time. The meaning of the tick, the dash and the cross should be taught once, in Land.

The second try is drawn in every level of the wireframes. In the film, six second tries would be slow and repetitive, because the first test has already shown all three plans. I suggest that the film shows the second try in one or two levels only. In Land, the far meadow would teach more as the second try than the river bank does, because it is the plan most children expect to be safe.

## Recommendations in order of priority

1. Make the child's sentence matter. Each of Florence's plans should visibly include what the child asked for, and the data should define what Florence offers for every button, including the weaker ones.
2. Stop the best buttons from naming the winning plan. The buttons should set a condition that all three plans then meet in different ways, so that the test is what separates them. Level 4 is closest to this already.
3. Repair the Homes level so that it teaches one thing. Either the test shows what the sun and the windows did, or the buttons become about materials. Correct the four science points listed above.
4. Make the Power test fair to its own lesson. The test needs a moment that separates a wind farm alone from wind, sun and storage together, and the battery needs to be seen filling.
5. Change each recap so that the child points to a cause. Give level 4 a tap. Accept both right answers in level 2 or change the question.
6. Run the tests one plan at a time, and show each check as its own moment. Remove or replace any check that the child does not see happen.
7. Bring the retry line, the two lines that mention a cost, and the discussion question into line with the rule that one plan is good on every check.
8. Speak every line aloud, introduce each check when it is first used, and shorten the opening screen.
9. Give level 6 a little more to do than level 1, so that the hour ends on its hardest thinking.
10. Fix the three contradictions in the story of the town before the film is drawn.
11. Build the film in chapters with pause points for predicting, voting and explaining.
12. Update the Eco Town paragraph of the curriculum and add three or four discussion aims to the end-of-hour screen.

## Points that need the owner's decision

1. What should Florence offer when a child taps a weaker word button? Today the data has an answer only for the best buttons. This decides whether "Ask again" has any purpose.
2. May the Power test include a moment with no wind, so that the wind farm alone is no longer good on every check and the mixed plan wins for the right reason? This changes one result from a tick to a dash or a cross.
3. May a check be removed from a level when all three plans score the same on it and the child does not see it tested? This affects "Staying warm" in Homes and "Clean air" in Power, and it would mean that level 5 has two checks and not three.
4. Should the Homes level be about where a home stands and faces, or about what it is made of? It cannot teach both with one test button.
5. Should the discussion question about what the child gave up be replaced, now that every level has a plan that is good on every check? The new words would come from the owner's writing process.
6. Should the questions about Florence stay, given that both are always answered yes? If they stay, the answer should be visible in the town.
7. Should the Eco Town paragraph in `docs/curriculum.md` be rewritten to remove "confidently wrong" and to name a skill for each level?
8. What should a child do who finishes the six levels in twenty minutes? Free play and extra challenges are both ruled out for now, so the choices are longer tests, a second town visit, or the break game that is already on the work queue.
9. Should the film show the second try in every level, as the wireframes do, or in one or two levels only?
10. Should a farmer be added to the jobs, so that the Food level has a job of its own?
