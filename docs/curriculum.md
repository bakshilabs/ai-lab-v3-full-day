# What each hour teaches: real jobs, real skills

*Approved by the user on 15 November 2026 ("agree. this is terrific."): the uses and jobs, the new titles, six missions of about seven minutes then the talk and the break, and all five modules in the day in this order. Being built.*

*The job lists were changed by the owner on 21 November 2026. They now use simple job names that a child knows, and only jobs that really do the work of that hour. The older lists named jobs such as data analyst and environmental scientist, which do not do this work.*

## What the user asked for (word for word)
> "extending each module from 10 minutes to 1 hour cannot be more of the same skill. each topic is extensive and complex, and there is a lot to teach in the form of a game. by playing the game, the students will learn real skills they can apply to future careers in robotics, or becoming a drone pilot, etc."
>
> "think of the real life applications of the technologies. drones are used for more than deliveries. robots are used for more than, sorting waste."

## How every hour is shaped
- **Six missions of about seven minutes, each a different real use of the technology,** each teaching a different real skill. Together they cover the job, not one task. The ten-minute loop already built becomes one of the six.
- **Each mission opens with the real job** in one line ("Search and rescue teams fly drones with heat cameras") and ends with what the child can now do.
- **The skills are the real ones, made simple, not made up.** Where a real rule exists, the game uses it (for drones, the UK Drone and Model Aircraft Code: fly below 120 metres, keep the drone in sight, keep away from people and airports).
- **Ages 13–17 get the numbers the job uses** (metres, kilowatt-hours, percentages, overlap, speed); ages 8–12 get the same ideas without them, and no money.
- **Florence is a capable partner,** not something to catch out: she helps, and the child decides.
- **Then "Talk it over" (10 to 15 minutes, teacher-led) and a break.** The last screen before the talk is **"Jobs that use this"**: three or four real jobs, each with one line on what the person does.
- Progress is saved after every mission; a child who stops early keeps what they finished.

## 1. Eco Town (already one hour)
Unchanged: planning a town with an AI that can be confidently wrong, and checking it with evidence. Jobs: town planner (decides where homes, streets and parks should go), architect (designs homes and schools), engineer (designs roads, bridges and power lines).

## 2. Drones: from delivery to rescue
| # | Real use | What the child does | Skill learnt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Every flight: ground school** | Go through a pre-flight check (battery, propellers, weather, wind); read an airspace map with no-fly zones around the school, the hospital and nesting birds. | Pre-flight checks; airspace rules from the real Drone Code; deciding "fly or don't fly". |
| 2 | **Hand flying** | Take off, hover still in a crosswind, fly through hoops, land on a pad. | Manual control; correcting for wind drift; smooth landings. |
| 3 | **Delivery** (built) | Fly a parcel by hand, then write the route as steps for the drone to fly by itself, within its battery. | Waypoint route planning; battery planning; turning a skill into instructions. |
| 4 | **Farm survey and mapping** | Plan a back-and-forth survey pattern over the farms so the photos overlap, then read the map to find the dry field and the sick crop. | Survey patterns; photo overlap; reading aerial data. |
| 5 | **Inspection** | Fly close to a wind turbine blade and the bridge, hold steady, find the crack, keep a safe distance. | Precision flying; inspection routine; safety distances. |
| 6 | **Search and rescue at dusk** | A walker is lost. Choose the heat camera, plan a search pattern, find them, and guide the rescue team. | Choosing the right sensor; search patterns; working as part of a team. |

Ages 13–17 also: battery in watt-hours against distance and wind, overlap as a percentage, the 120-metre height limit on a side view.
Jobs: drone pilot (flies drones safely), map maker (uses photographs from the air to draw maps), bridge inspector (checks bridges and towers for damage), rescue worker (searches for people who are lost).

## 3. Robots: sense, think, act
| # | Real use | What the child does | Skill learnt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **How any robot works** | Match sensors to jobs (camera, distance sensor, bump switch, thermometer) and watch a robot sense, decide and act. | The sense–think–act loop; what each sensor can and cannot tell. |
| 2 | **Factory and greenhouse robot arm** | Program an arm with a sequence (move to, open, close, lift) to pick and pack seedlings, then use a repeat to do a whole tray. | Sequences, positions, loops; debugging a program step by step. |
| 3 | **Hospital delivery robot** | Write rules for a robot taking medicine across the clinic: slow near people, stop for an obstacle, take the lift. | Rules with "if"; navigation; safety around people. |
| 4 | **Recycling sorter** (built) | Teach a robot by showing it examples, test it, show it what it has not seen yet. | Training by examples; testing on unseen things. |
| 5 | **Farm robot** | Teach a weeding robot crop from weed; decide how sure it must be before it acts, because pulling a crop costs more than missing a weed. | Mistakes have different costs; a confidence threshold. |
| 6 | **Flood rescue robot** | Design a robot for a flooded street: body (wheels, tracks, legs, boat), sensors, power. Test it in a simulation and improve it. | Engineering design trade-offs; test, then improve. |

Ages 13–17 also: coordinates for the arm, a confusion table for the sorter and the farm robot, battery life against weight.
Jobs: robot engineer (designs and builds robots), robot mechanic (keeps robots working and mends them), farmer (grows food, sometimes with the help of machines), tester (checks that a machine does its job properly).

## 4. Self-driving cars and the street
| # | Real use | What the child does | Skill learnt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **How a self-driving car sees** | Switch between camera, radar and lidar views of the same street, in sun, rain, fog and dark; find what each one misses. | Sensors and their limits; why cars use several. |
| 2 | **Teaching the car to recognise things** | Label street scenes (person, cyclist, dog, sign); find the hard ones (a person half hidden, a wheelchair, a reflection). | Labelling data; edge cases. |
| 3 | **Rules of the road** | Set what the car does at a crossing, near the school and behind a cyclist: speed, gap, who goes first. | Priorities and safe gaps; turning rules into behaviour. |
| 4 | **Testing in simulation** | Run the car through a rainy morning and the school rush, find where it fails, change one setting at a time. | Testing in simulation; changing one thing at a time. |
| 5 | **A shared fleet** | Choose how many shared cars, where they wait and charge, and see waiting times and empty trips. | Fleet planning; reading the numbers. |
| 6 | **Redesigning the street** (built) | Redesign the street for shared cars and choose what the old car park becomes. | Designing for everyone who uses a street. |

Ages 13–17 also: stopping distance against speed and rain, the trade between waiting time and number of cars.
Jobs: car engineer (designs cars and the parts that help them see), transport planner (decides how streets, buses and crossings should work), test driver (tries out new cars to check that they are safe), road safety officer (looks for ways to make streets safer).

## 5. Town film studio: making media with AI
| # | Real use | What the child does | Skill learnt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The film crew's language** | Learn wide, medium and close shots, and pan, tilt and track, by matching shots to what a scene needs. | Shot types and camera moves. |
| 2 | **Storyboarding** | Put shots in order to tell a story with a beginning, middle and end. | Story structure; planning before filming. |
| 3 | **Directing an AI helper** (built) | Brief Florence, watch her first cut, improve it with feedback. | Briefing clearly; improving a draft through feedback. |
| 4 | **Editing** | Trim, reorder and pace the cut; choose music and where it changes. | Editing rhythm; how sound sets the mood. |
| 5 | **Making it for everyone** | Add captions and a described version for someone who cannot see the screen. | Accessibility in media. |
| 6 | **True or made up?** | Spot which shots were changed or made by a machine; check permission before filming people. | Checking media; consent and honesty. |

Jobs: director (decides how a film tells its story), film editor (chooses which shots to keep and puts them in order), camera operator (films the shots), caption writer (adds words to a film for people who cannot hear it).

## 6. Power control room: keeping the lights on
| # | Real use | What the child does | Skill learnt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The grid must balance** | Keep supply level with demand minute by minute as the town wakes up. | Supply and demand; why balance matters. |
| 2 | **Forecasting** | Read the wind and sun forecast and say when power will be short. | Reading forecasts and charts. |
| 3 | **Storage** | Decide when to charge and use the town battery through a day. | Storage and timing. |
| 4 | **Automation rules** (built) | Write rules the system follows by itself; test them on three days. | Writing rules for an automated system. |
| 5 | **Smart demand** | Move flexible jobs (car charging, washing) to the hours with spare clean power. | Demand response; working with people's needs. |
| 6 | **Storm night** | A storm cuts a line. Decide who gets power first (the hospital, the school shelter) and restore the town in order. | Priorities in an emergency; restoring a system safely. |

Ages 13–17 also: kilowatts against kilowatt-hours, a supply and demand chart, battery percentage thresholds.
Jobs: power engineer (keeps electricity flowing to homes), wind turbine technician (looks after wind turbines), solar panel fitter (puts solar panels on roofs), emergency planner (decides what to do when something breaks).
