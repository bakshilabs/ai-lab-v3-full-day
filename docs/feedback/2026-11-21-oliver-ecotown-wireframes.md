# EcoTown –  Feedback

Scope: EcoTown only. This document focuses on the changes needed to make the first game clearer, more active and more useful for children aged 8–12.

# 1\. Changes that matter most

1\) Turn the checks into visible tests. Children should see the river rise, people walk different routes, homes warm up, bees and birds respond, and power change during winter or low wind. Ratings such as “Good” and “Poor” should support the evidence, not replace it.  
2\) Add a friendly retry loop. After each result, Florence should say: “Your plan works in some ways. Shall we go back and see what happens with another plan?” Children should be able to test another option before they commit.  
3\) Add a short recap or quiz after every level. The recap should make the ecology lesson explicit and gradually explain the AI lesson.  
4\) Explain Florence clearly. At the start of EcoTown, say: “Florence is a computer helper. She can suggest ideas, but you must check whether they work.” This makes the AI literacy purpose visible without making every activity about AI.  
5\) Vary the activity. Keep the sentence-building mechanic as a way to ask Florence, but make the child’s main activity different in each level: place, route, build, grow, power and audit.  
6\) Use “plan” rather than “solution” where possible. A child is choosing between possible plans, not finding one correct answer.

# 2\. Feedback by EcoTown level

Land  
This is a good first level because the consequence is easy to understand. The child should place the town or choose between visible locations, then run a rain-and-river test. The child should be able to go back and try the other location. The learning point is that a computer suggestion still needs to be checked against evidence.

Getting around  
This is one of the strongest trade-off levels. Do not only show walking ratings. Animate different people using the routes and show how much space is left for nature. The child should discover that a route can be short for one group and longer for another. The learning point is that a plan should be tested from more than one person’s point of view.

Homes  
The choice between materials is useful, but the environmental explanations need to stay accurate and simple. Show a cold night and let the child see which homes stay warm and how much energy they use. Avoid suggesting that trees simply “clean the air” or that concrete “gives off fumes”; explain that different materials have different pollution and warmth costs.

Food  
This is currently the best example of the intended AI interaction. The child adds detail, and Florence gives more suitable suggestions. Make that lesson explicit: “You gave Florence more details, so her suggestions changed. You still need to test them.” Show bees, birds, water and walking routes changing as the child tests the plans.

Power  
The winter and low-wind problem is a good reason to test a plan rather than simply choose the cleanest-looking option. Show the town at night or during a still winter day. Clarify that a battery stores power; it is not itself a power source. Replace the vague idea that a battery “causes fumes” with a simple explanation that making a large battery causes some pollution.

A school for everyone  
This works well as the final EcoTown challenge because the child must choose what matters most and give a reason. Show children from the centre and the farms walking to each possible school, and test what happens when the river rises. The final learning point should be that people decide what matters most, while Florence helps compare the plans.

# 3\. Recaps to add after each level

Land: “What should you check before building near a river?” Answer: what happens when the river rises.  
Getting around: “Why should we test a route with different people?” Answer: one route can help some people and make things harder for others.  
Homes: “What can change how much energy a home needs?” Answer: its materials and how well it keeps heat in.  
Food: “What changed when you gave Florence more details?” Answer: her suggestions changed, but they still needed to be checked.  
Power: “Why should we test power on a winter day?” Answer: there may be less sun or wind, so the plan may need another source or stored power.  
School: “Who decides what matters most in the town?” Answer: the people in the town; Florence can help compare the choices.

# 4\. End-of-hour screen

Keep the question: “Which choice was the hardest to make in EcoTown, and what did you give up when you made it?” It is a strong discussion prompt.  
Keep Town planner, Environmental scientist and Data analyst as teacher-led job examples. They are useful for follow-up discussion; the teacher can explain Data analyst if needed. No wording change is essential here.

# 5\. Wording changes

The table below includes only wording changes that I recommend. It does not suggest rewriting every screen.

| Level / screen | Current wording | Recommended wording | Why |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| All offer screens | Here are the solutions I can offer. Every one of them would work. | Here are some possible plans. Let’s test what happens with each one. | Avoids implying that the child has already found a correct answer. |
| All compare / choose screens | Look at how each solution does in each check. Choose the solution that you think is best for the town. | Test each plan and watch what happens. Choose the plan you think is best for the town. | Uses active, child-friendly language and makes “plan” consistent. |
| Opening, s2 | This is a low town of homes, farms and a river. | This is a small town with homes, farms and a river. | “Low town” is unclear; “small town” is natural here. |
| Opening, s2 | CHECK THIS TOWN FOR SIX THINGS | TEST THE TOWN IN SIX WAYS | Signals that the child will actively test the town. |
| Opening, s2 | Sharing. This check shows whether everyone gets a share. | Similar walking distances. This check shows whether everyone walks a similar distance to the places they need to reach. | Explains the actual idea; “sharing” does not describe walking distances. |
| Opening, s2 | Staying warm. This check shows whether homes are warm and bright on a cold, dark day. | Staying warm. This check shows whether homes stay warm on a cold day. | Keeps the check about warmth rather than mixing warmth with lighting. |
| Land, s3 | Eco Town needs a place to stand. Ask Florence to find land for the town. | Choose where to build Eco Town. Ask Florence to suggest two places. | Gives the child a clear action and makes Florence’s role clearer. |
| Land, s4 | Find land for the town that is safe from floods. | Choose land for the town that stays safe when the river rises. | Connects the words to the visible rain-and-river test. |
| Land, s5 | Here are the solutions I can offer. Every one of them would work. | Use the global wording above: possible plans, then test what happens with each one. | The child should discover the result rather than be told that every option works. |
| Getting around, s9 | Ask Florence to plan the paths and streets. | Ask Florence to suggest paths and streets. | Makes the child’s role and Florence’s role distinct. |
| Getting around, s10 | Plan the streets so that everyone can reach the shops on foot. | Plan the streets so people can reach the shops on foot and leave space for nature. | Makes the central trade-off visible in the child’s request. |
| Homes, s17 | Concrete homes are made from poured concrete blocks. | Concrete homes are made from concrete. | Removes the confusing “poured blocks” wording. |
| Homes, s18 | Timber: growing trees clean the air. Brick: brick ovens give off some smoke. Concrete: cement works give off many fumes. | Timber: making these homes causes less air pollution in this town. Brick: making the bricks creates some pollution. Concrete: making concrete creates more pollution. | Keeps the comparison simple without making misleading claims about trees, ovens or fumes. |
| Homes, s20 | The timber homes keep the air the cleanest. | The timber homes cause the least air pollution in this town. | More direct and easier to understand. |
| Food, s22 | Place the farms near the town and protect the wildlife. | Place farms close enough for people to reach, while protecting wildlife. | Makes the walking and wildlife trade-off explicit. |
| Food, s24 | Look at how each solution does in each check. | Watch the bees, birds, water and walking routes as you test each plan. | Tells the child what evidence to look for. |
| Food, s26 | You told me more, so I could fit the job more closely. | You gave me more details, so I could suggest plans that fit your town better. | Makes the prompt-and-response AI lesson explicit. |
| Food, s27 | The new results are better overall, and there is still a real choice to make. | The new plans fit your extra details. Test them again. | Does not tell the child that the new results are already better. |
| Power, s31 | Power the town with clean energy that keeps going in winter. | Power the town with clean energy that still works on winter days. | More natural wording for younger children. |
| Power, s33 | Choose the three checks that matter most for this job. | Choose three things to test first. | Keeps the prioritisation but makes the instruction easier. |
| Power, s34/s36 | Making a big battery causes fumes. | Making a big battery causes some pollution. | Clearer and less likely to suggest that the battery produces smoke while running. |
| School, s38 | Build a school for every family that is easy to walk to and is safe from floods. | Choose a school site that is easy for everyone to reach and stays safe when the river rises. | More concrete and connected to the visible test. |
| School, s40 | Choose the three checks that matter most for this job. | Choose three things to test before you decide where to build the school. | Explains why the child is choosing the checks. |
| School, s41 | Look at how each solution does in each check. | Watch children walk to each school and see what happens when the river rises. | Replaces abstract ratings with visible evidence. |
| School, s43 | Choose the reason that is closest to your own. | Choose the reason that best matches your choice. | More natural phrasing for children. |
